Employment protection legislation (EPL) deregulation has expanded labor market flexibility across advanced economies, with underexplored consequences for population health. This paper asks whether such deregulation contributes to deaths of despair (drug overdoses, alcohol-related disease, and suicide), a mortality pattern first documented in the United States. Using OECD employment protection indicators and WHO mortality data for 26 economies from 1998&ndash;2019, I apply local projections to trace the dynamic mortality response to major EPL reforms. Drug use disorder and suicide mortality rise significantly within five years of deregulation, with no significant rise in alcohol-related mortality. Some of these responses are moderated by institutional context: health-system effectiveness attenuates drug and inflammatory heart disease mortality; income support protects against suicide and violence mortality but, unexpectedly, amplifies the alcohol-mortality response; and higher poverty dampens the drug-mortality response, consistent with an affordability channel. As suggestive evidence on mechanisms, net family income for low-wage families falls and the short-tenure employment share rises after major deregulation. These findings indicate that labor market deregulation carries public-health costs, which social safety nets and health-system capacity can mitigate.
