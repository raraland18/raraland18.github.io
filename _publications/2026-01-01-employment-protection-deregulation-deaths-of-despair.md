Income and wealth inequality have risen sharply within advanced economies, raising concerns about their social and health consequences. This paper examines whether labor market deregulation&mdash;specifically employment protection legislation (EPL) reforms that weaken workers' bargaining power&mdash;contributes to deaths of despair (drug overdoses, alcohol-related disease, and suicide). Using OECD employment protection indicators and WHO mortality data for 26 advanced economies from 1998&ndash;2019, I estimate the dynamic effects of major EPL reforms on age-standardized death rates via Jordà's (2005) local projection method. Results show a significant rise in deaths from drug use disorders and suicide within five years of major EPL reforms. Government social transfers, unemployment benefit replacement rates, public health coverage, avoidable mortality, and poverty levels all mediate these effects, consistent with an institutional-buffering mechanism. These findings highlight the health risks of labor market deregulation and the need for policies that strengthen social safety nets to mitigate deaths of despair.
